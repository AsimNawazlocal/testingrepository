How to tell if it's an emergency

Not everything needs a same-hour crew, and we'd rather say so on the phone than run up a bill you didn't need.

  • Call now: active water loss you can't stop, a gas smell near an appliance, or a commercial space that can't open with the plumbing down.
  • Schedule it: a remodel project, a new ADU rough-in, or a repair that isn't actively getting worse.

What to do before we arrive

  • Find your home's main water shutoff, usually near the water meter, and turn it off if water is actively leaking.
  • If you smell gas near an appliance, leave the building and call us from outside.
  • For a remodel or new-construction job, have your plans or permit paperwork ready so we can scope the work accurately.

How a dispatch call works

  • 1. Phone triage: tell us what's happening and we sort it into emergency, same-day, or scheduled.
  • 2. Scope and quote: for remodel, new-construction, or commercial work, we confirm scope before anyone drives out.
  • 3. On-site diagnosis: we confirm the problem matches what was described and flag anything that changes the quote before starting.
  • 4. Sign-off: nothing proceeds until you've approved the work and the price.

Why Sandy's older and newer homes differ here

Sandy's historic core near Main and Center Streets dates to the 1870s, while most of the city's housing was built out through the 1970s and 80s suburban boom. A remodel on an older home near the core more often uncovers galvanized supply lines or cast-iron drains that need updating as part of the job; newer construction is usually just extending modern PEX and ABS.

Permits on remodel & new-construction work

Rerouting supply or drain lines during a remodel, or rough-in work on a new ADU, typically requires a Sandy building permit and inspection. We flag this up front rather than leave it for you to discover later, and we can schedule the inspection as part of the job.

What doesn't count as a true emergency

Not every urgent-feeling problem needs an after-hours dispatch. A slow drain, a running toilet, or a dripping faucet can safely wait for a normal business-hours appointment, and holding it until then costs less than an emergency call-out. We'll tell you plainly on the phone if what you're describing can wait, rather than dispatch an after-hours technician for something that doesn't need one.

What does warrant the emergency line: active flooding, no water at all to the home, a gas smell, a sewage backup inside the house, or a burst pipe you can't shut off. If you're not sure which category you're in, call anyway and describe it — that's a faster way to find out than guessing on your own.

After-hours versus same-day: what changes

A same-day daytime call and an after-hours emergency dispatch use the same technicians and the same firm-quote-before-work process. What changes is availability and routing: after-hours calls get priority dispatch ahead of scheduled work, since the whole point of the emergency line is getting someone there before a manageable problem becomes a bigger one.

  • Daytime, non-urgent: scheduled into the day's route, usually same day or next.
  • After-hours, active problem: dispatched immediately, ahead of scheduled stops.

Why dispatch asks so many questions up front

A good dispatcher asks several questions before scheduling anything, not to slow you down but to send the right technician with the right parts on the first trip. Whether it's a gas smell versus a water leak, whether the main shutoff is already closed, and how long the problem has been happening all change who gets sent and what they bring. A call that skips this step often turns into a second visit once the real scope becomes clear on site.

This matters more for emergency calls than scheduled ones, since there's no time for a callback-and-reschedule cycle when water is actively running. Answering a few extra questions on the phone is almost always faster than a technician arriving unprepared for what's actually wrong.
