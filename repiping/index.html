Whole-House Repiping & Pipe Replacement in Grapevine: The Basics

A whole-house repipe replaces every supply line in the home, not just the one that failed. We recommend it when a house keeps patching the same kind of leak, when water pressure has dropped house-wide rather than at one fixture, or when a line opened up for an unrelated repair turns out corroded well beyond that one spot. Patching a single failure on a pipe nearing the end of its service life usually just delays the next one.

Galvanized steel pipe, common in homes built before the mid-1960s, corrodes from the inside out. The outside of the pipe can look fine while the inside has narrowed down to a fraction of its original diameter, which shows up as weak pressure throughout the house well before a visible leak does. Copper fails differently, usually as a pinhole leak from pitting, often where water sits in a line that does not get used often.

PEX is the standard replacement material on most repipes today. It runs through the attic and down through interior walls rather than through the slab, which means the job does not require breaking concrete the way a slab leak repair sometimes does. Some households choose copper again for their own reasons, and we discuss that choice at the estimate rather than assume it.

The job runs as a reroute, new lines run overhead and the old ones abandoned in place or capped, not a line-by-line swap inside the existing walls. Drywall opens only where a new line has to come down to a fixture, and we map out those cuts at the estimate so there are no surprise openings once the work is underway.

A typical single-story home finishes in two to three days. A two-story home, or one with a complicated fixture layout, can run three to five days, since drywall access from above does not reach every fixture the same way it does on a single level. Water gets shut off section by section as the work moves through the house rather than for the entire job at once, so the household is rarely without any water for a full day.

A whole-house repipe runs $4,500 to $11,000 depending on square footage, number of bathrooms, and whether it is a single-story slab home or a two-story home with more drywall to open and close. We confirm that range, and what specifically drives a given home toward either end of it, at the walk-through before anyone signs a contract.

Factors Specific to This Area

Grapevine's older homes, concentrated around Historic Main Street and the neighborhoods that grew up around the original townsite, are more likely to still carry galvanized supply lines than the newer construction further from downtown. A house from that era that has not been repiped yet is usually not far from needing it, and a pressure test during an unrelated service call often turns up the first real evidence of it.

The same hard water that shortens a water heater's service life here also accelerates corrosion inside galvanized pipe, since mineral buildup narrows an already-corroding line faster than it would in an area with softer water. A repipe conversation sometimes starts as a water heater or softener consultation once a technician notices pressure or corrosion that points to the supply lines rather than the appliance.

On Arrival

A repipe estimate starts with a walk-through of the attic and the fixture layout, not a quote given over the phone, since the drywall access and routing depend on how the specific house is framed. The plan, which lines move first, which rooms lose water on which day, and where drywall will need to be opened and patched, gets laid out before the first cut is made. A rough-in inspection happens under Grapevine's current plumbing code before the new lines are closed up behind drywall, and that inspection is built into the schedule rather than treated as an afterthought.

A Few Things to Do First

  • Clear a path to the attic access and to any crawlspace or utility closet the crew will need to reach.
  • Note which fixtures already drip or run slow, since that history helps prioritize the rerouting plan.
  • Move furniture and stored items away from walls on the side where new lines are likely to run.
  • Plan for water being shut off to one section of the house at a time, not the entire home, on each day of the job.
